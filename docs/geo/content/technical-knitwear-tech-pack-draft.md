# What to Include in a Tech Pack for Technical Knitwear

A tech pack for cut-and-sew technical performance knitwear should define the
product, finished fabric, measurements, seam map, stitch construction, trims,
testing requirements and approval process in enough detail for development and
bulk production to use the same reference. A sketch and a size chart alone are
not sufficient.

The document should answer three questions without relying on assumptions:

1. What is being made?
2. How must it perform and be constructed?
3. What evidence will be used to approve the sample and bulk production?

This guide uses “technical knitwear” to mean garments cut and sewn from knitted
fabrics for sportswear, underwear, base layers and outdoor applications. It is
not a specification for fully fashioned or whole-garment flat knitting, which
requires a different set of machine and knitting-program instructions.

## 1. Start with document control and intended use

Every page should identify the style number, product name, season or program,
size range, document version, issue date and responsible approver. A revision
history should state what changed, when it changed and which sample or purchase
order the revision applies to.

The intended use also matters. A close-fitting base layer, compression garment,
running top and casual knit T-shirt may use similar silhouettes but require
different stretch, recovery, seam placement and testing. Record the target
activity, fit, layering position, expected skin contact and relevant pressure
zones before specifying construction.

Commercial assumptions that affect development should be stated separately,
including estimated quantity, color split, size ratio, sales market and target
delivery window. These fields do not replace the purchase order, but they help
the manufacturer review feasibility and material requirements early.

## 2. Provide technical flats and a construction callout page

Include clear front, back and relevant inside views. Add enlarged drawings for
the neckline, armhole, gusset, waistband, pocket, zipper, hem, elastic join,
binding and any unusual panel intersection.

Use numbered callouts that connect the drawing to a construction table. Each
callout should identify the operation rather than relying on a general note
such as “flat seams throughout.” If the inside appearance matters, show both
faces of the approved seam.

For asymmetrical products, state which view represents the wearer's left or
right side. For placement-sensitive products, identify the reference point from
which each artwork, pocket or trim position is measured.

## 3. Define POMs, measurement method and tolerances

The point-of-measurement table should contain:

- POM code and description.
- Diagram showing the measurement location.
- Base-size measurement.
- Grade rules or measurements for every size.
- Tolerance for each POM.
- Whether the garment is measured relaxed, stretched or under another stated
  condition.
- Any pre-wash and after-wash measurement requirements.

Avoid ambiguous terms such as “body length” without showing the start and end
points. Curved seams, elastic openings and stretch panels need a stated
measurement method because different handling can produce different results.

## 4. Specify the finished fabric, not only the fiber composition

“Polyester/spandex jersey” does not identify a production fabric. The bill of
materials and fabric specification should record, where applicable:

- Approved supplier and finished-fabric article number.
- Fiber composition and permitted tolerance or verification requirement.
- Knit construction.
- Finished weight in GSM and the agreed test method.
- Usable width.
- Stretch and recovery in the relevant directions, with the test method,
  conditioning and acceptance values.
- Finished hand, surface, brushing or fleece requirement.
- Color standard, shade approval method and lot-control requirement.
- Functional finish and its test method, where a finish is required.
- Dimensional-change, spirality, colorfastness, pilling or bursting-strength
  requirements relevant to the end use.

The test method must suit the material. For example,
[ASTM D2594/D2594M-21](https://store.astm.org/d2594_d2594m-21.html) covers
stretch and growth of certain low-power knitted fabrics and explicitly notes
limitations for support applications. A buyer should not copy one stretch
method into every program without checking its scope.

If a recycled, organic or other certified-material claim is required, specify
the exact claim, applicable product and required documentation. A supplier or
upstream material certificate does not automatically prove that the final
garment and transaction can carry the same claim.

## 5. Draw a seam map and identify stitch construction

A seam map should label every joining and finishing operation. It should
distinguish, where applicable:

- 607 four-needle, six-thread FLATLOCK.
- 514 two-needle, four-thread OVERLOCK.
- ACTIVESEAM as a separate Merrow construction rather than a generic name for
  any visually flat seam.
- COVERSTITCH for relevant hems or top-cover operations.
- Binding, elastic attachment, bartack or other reinforcement operations.

For each critical seam, state the location, stitch reference, required seam
width, seam allowance where relevant, thread specification, stitch density or
SPI where applicable, and approved appearance on both faces. Attach a close-up
photo or signed seam sample when appearance cannot be communicated adequately
by a drawing.

The seam map should also consider pressure and movement. A low-profile seam may
be preferred beneath a backpack strap or at a high-contact next-to-skin join,
but the final choice still depends on the fabric, fit, thread, seam geometry and
approved sample. See the separate
[FLATLOCK vs OVERLOCK guide](https://www.athletikapparel.com/flatlock-vs-overlock-technical-knitwear/) for
the construction comparison.

## 6. Control trims, elastic and component compatibility

List every component in the bill of materials with supplier or article details
where nominated. Include elastic width and extension requirements, zipper type
and length, drawcord, stopper, label, transfer, reflective component, thread,
padding and packaging items.

Identify attachment and joining requirements for components that affect fit or
durability. For elastic, define the finished opening measurement or extension
relationship and approve the join and attachment on the production fabric.
For heat-applied graphics, identify placement, artwork revision, finished size,
color standard and any laundering or adhesion requirement.

## 7. State testing methods and pass criteria

A requirement such as “must pass shrinkage” is incomplete. State the test
method, specimen or garment condition, wash and dry procedure, number of
cycles, measurement method and acceptance value.

[AATCC TM135-2025](https://members.aatcc.org/store/tm135/543/) is intended for
dimensional changes of fabrics after home laundering.
[ISO 3759:2011](https://www.iso.org/standard/57309.html) covers preparation,
marking and measurement of fabrics and garments for dimensional-change tests,
while [ISO 5077:2007](https://www.iso.org/standard/41877.html) covers the
determination of dimensional change after specified washing and drying. These
references demonstrate why the procedure and measurement basis must accompany
the pass value.

Depending on the product risk, the test plan may address:

- Fabric weight, stretch and recovery.
- Dimensional change and garment twist after laundering.
- Colorfastness to washing, perspiration or rubbing.
- Pilling, abrasion or bursting strength.
- Seam extension, seam strength or seam appearance after laundering.
- Print or transfer adhesion.
- Functional claims that will be used in product marketing.

Do not add every possible test to every program. Select methods and acceptance
values according to the fabric, garment, end use, target market and buyer's
quality standard.

## 8. Define sample stages and approval authority

The tech pack should state which samples are required and what each approval
allows the manufacturer to do next. A typical sequence may include development
or proto sample, fit sample, size set, color or material approval and
pre-production sample. The exact sequence depends on the project.

Record comments against the current sample and tech pack version. When an
approved change affects pattern, fabric, seam construction, trim, artwork or
measurement, update the controlled document rather than relying on email or
chat history alone.

Bulk production should use the approved pre-production sample, signed material
standards and current tech pack as a coordinated reference. If they conflict,
the responsible parties should resolve the conflict before cutting.

## 9. Final handoff checklist

Before requesting a quotation or sample, confirm that the package contains:

1. Style identity, intended use and current revision.
2. Front, back, inside and detail drawings.
3. POM diagrams, graded measurements and tolerances.
4. BOM with finished-fabric and component specifications.
5. Color, artwork, label and placement files.
6. Seam map and critical stitch construction.
7. Testing methods and pass criteria.
8. Packaging and shipment-marking requirements.
9. Sample stages and named approval authority.
10. Quantity, color/size assumptions and target timing for feasibility review.

The goal is not to create the longest possible document. It is to remove
material ambiguity before sampling and to keep every approved change connected
to one controlled product specification.

## Common buyer questions

### Does the buyer need to specify every machine setting?

No. The buyer should define the required construction, location, appearance and
performance. The manufacturer can propose needle, thread, tension, differential
feed and other settings during development, then record and control the approved
result for bulk production.

### Can one tech pack cover several fabrics?

Only if the differences are clearly controlled. A change in fabric weight,
stretch, recovery, thickness or edge stability may require different pattern,
seam or testing decisions. Record material-specific requirements rather than
assuming one approval applies to every fabric option.

### Should testing values be supplied by the manufacturer?

The manufacturer may recommend methods or achievable values, but the buyer
should approve the final acceptance criteria for the intended product and
market. Marketing claims and regulatory requirements remain the responsibility
of the parties using those claims.

### What causes the most avoidable sampling delays?

Common causes include missing finished-fabric details, unclear measurement
points, conflicting drawings and tables, unspecified seam locations, artwork
without placement dimensions, and comments that are not incorporated into the
next controlled revision.
